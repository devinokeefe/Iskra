# Document 449: the address at the end of Lepeshinsky's letter, 1902

Some time before 6 June 1902 Panteleimon Lepeshinsky, one of *Iskra*'s agents, wrote from Pskov to its
editorial board, whose office had moved from Munich to London in April. He expected to travel abroad with
Elizaveta Vasilyevna, the mother of Nadezhda Krupskaya, and he had a man to send to the board as well.
Krupskaya was Lenin's wife and kept the board's correspondence. Towards the end of the letter he asks:
«Сообщите, дорогие друзья, ваш личный адрес, куда к вам держать путь. [...] Только поспешите с адресом.»
("Let us have your personal address, dear friends, so that we know where to make for. [...] Only be quick
with the address.") In the Soviet edition of the correspondence ([Sources](../README.md#sources)) three lines
of number pairs follow in the same paragraph, printed without a reading, and A. V. Sinelnikov lists them
among the cryptograms that are still unread. Read with the key of the rest of the letter, they give the
address that Lepeshinsky had asked for:

> Якоб Рихтер, Холфорд-сквер 30, Пентонвил, Лондон В. С.

That is Jacob Richter, 30 Holford Square, Pentonville, London W.C. Jacob Richter was the name Lenin used in
London, and 30 Holford Square was where he and Krupskaya lodged from April 1902. The index of the edition
gives both under «Richter J.», and Lenin sent the address to P. B. Axelrod on 23 April. The compilers took
the address missing from Krupskaya's reply to be "probably J. Richter's" (see below), and the pairs
confirm the guess.

## How it reads

The key is a poem, as it was for many of *Iskra*'s correspondents, and a pair such as 15.3 means the third
letter of the fifteenth line, counting from the second printed line of the poem. The poem here is Martov's
"Hymn of the Newest Russian Socialist", printed over the name Nartsis Tuporylov in the first number of *Zarya*
(Stuttgart, April 1901), the journal of the *Iskra* group. Sinelnikov names it as the key of this letter, and
as the key of five letters between the board and Inna Leman, formerly the board's secretary and by then an
agent in Russia (vol. 1, nos 268, 277, 279, 287 and 299). In three of them, nos 268, 287 and 299, the
compilers of the edition read passages without naming the key. The passage in no. 277 is printed as Krupskaya
deciphered it, apart from a surname that the compilers read, and the words in cipher in no. 279, from
Krupskaya to Leman, as the Police Department deciphered them. In January 1902 Leman told the board that
"2а3б", whom the index of the edition identifies as Lepeshinsky, was changing his key. He would take her more
recent key with the board, count from its second line and drop the first altogether, and keep to it until the
board changed the key, when he would move to the new one.

The table reads the first word pair by pair, with the letter given by each pair in brackets.

| pair | line of the poem (counted from the second) | letter |
|---|---|---|
| 1.6 | Темны[я] силы въ загривокъ насъ бьютъ, | я |
| 2.5 | Рабс[к]ія спины покрыты рубцами, | к |
| 11.4 | Раб[о]чій народъ! | о |
| 2.3 | Ра[б]скія спины покрыты рубцами, | б |

The whole run of 40 pairs follows in order, with the letter of each pair beneath it.

The 38 pairs after the first two give the address letter for letter. The Ф, the dash and the 30 are written
in clear, as ordinary letters and figures among the pairs, in the edition as they are here. The poem has no
ф in it, so that letter could not be given as a pair, and «Уфу», earlier in the same letter, has its Ф in
clear in the same way. Sinelnikov's count of 43 for this passage evidently includes those three. The
spelling is the cipher's: «Пентонвил» has one л, and W.C. is written as the two Russian letters В and С.

## Why it had not been read

Sinelnikov also records how Lepeshinsky counted. The refrain is printed as four short lines, and Lepeshinsky
took the last two, «Тише впередъ, / Рабочій народъ!», as one. Counted his way, the seven names in this letter
that Krupskaya deciphered at the time all read correctly: 38 of their 43 pairs give the letters, and the other
five are spare (see Limits). The address does not read with his count, and Sinelnikov noted that the key to
the rest of the letter "does not fit here". It reads in full when the refrain is counted as the four lines
that *Zarya* prints. From line 11 onwards the two counts differ by one line, and after the second refrain by
two; each reads one part of the letter and spoils the other, as the table shows.

| | the seven names (38 pairs) | the address (38 pairs) |
|---|---|---|
| lines as Lepeshinsky counted them | 38 right | 19 right |
| lines as printed in *Zarya* | 24 right | 38 right |

It is a coincidence that both columns have 38 pairs. The run of pairs has 40, and its first two belong to no
word of the address and are not counted.

## Who wrote the pairs

It is unlikely that Lepeshinsky wrote them. He was asking for the address, and the pairs are not counted in
the way he counted the names a few lines above.

They may have been written for the reply. Krupskaya answered on 6 June (no. 450 in the edition, an autograph
in the same archival file): they will be very glad to see him, he is to telegraph so that he can be met, and
if anything goes wrong with the telegram he should take a cab and give the driver «адрес, записанный на
бумажке», the address written on a slip of paper. Between the first two of these sentences stand the words
«Наш личный адрес» with nothing after them, and the compilers note there «Адрес не указан, вероятно
J. Richter'a» ("address not given, probably J. Richter's"). On this view someone in London enciphered the
address for that reply and wrote the pairs on the letter that was being answered, and the edition prints them
as the end of Lepeshinsky's text. Sinelnikov concludes from Lepeshinsky's way of counting that he enciphered
from a handwritten copy of the hymn, and the address follows the lines of the journal as printed, which is
what the board in London would have had before it.

The edition's rules print whatever is in Lenin's hand in bold and note Krupskaya's marks on letters in
footnotes (vol. 1, p. 24), and its footnotes name other people's additions to a letter where the compilers
could identify them, among them one that they give to the letter's addressee (vol. 1, no. 296, p. 369).
Nothing of the kind is noted at the pairs on no. 449. That tells against Lenin's or Krupskaya's hand and
against any hand that the compilers knew. A hand that they could not tell from Lepeshinsky's would have gone
without such a note. Krupskaya, moreover, deciphered Lepeshinsky's seven names by his count, so a reply meant
for him would be expected to follow that count; the address follows the journal's.

## Limits

- The first two pairs, 10.2 and 2.2, give и and а under either count. They stand before the name and have
  not been explained.
- Lepeshinsky's seven names have spare pairs too, five in all, which give the hard sign or point
  past the end of a line. Sinelnikov takes them for dummies.
- Only the manuscript can show whether the pairs are in another hand: ЦПА ИМЛ (the former Central Party
  Archive, now РГАСПИ), ф. 2, оп. 1, ед. хр. 769, л. 18 об. The legend of no. 449 calls it an original
  (подлинник), one of the kinds of document that the edition's rules distinguish (vol. 1, p. 24), and calls
  Krupskaya's reply an autograph (автограф). Lepeshinsky's other letters printed from the originals, nos 9 and
  324 in vol. 1, are called originals as well, so the term is the one that the edition uses for his letters
  and says nothing about the pairs in particular.

## Checking

From this folder:

    python code/decode.py --check

The script reads the poem and the pairs from `data/`, deciphers the run of pairs and the seven footnoted names
under both counts, and tests the run as a whole, the letters in clear included, and the figures and tables of
this page that [runs/check.txt](../runs/check.txt) names. As a control it also counts from each of the other
35 printed lines of the hymn as line 1, going on from the first line where the count runs past the last, and
the run then gives at most 8 of its 38 letters (from line 18). Without `--check` it prints the letters.

## Files

- `data/hymn.txt`: the hymn as *Zarya* prints it, one printed line to a line.
- `data/pairs.tsv`: the run of pairs and the seven footnoted names, with the pairs as printed.
- `code/decode.py`: the decipherment under both counts, and the checks.
